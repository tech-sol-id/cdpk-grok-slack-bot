What Is An Explosion Protection Document (EPD)
ATEX directive 1999/92/EC requires employers, who 
have processes or buildings in which explosive 
atmospheres can arise, to perform a risk analysis to 
demonstrate compliance by completing a formal 
documented risk assessment. 
An Explosion Protection Document (EPD) is created to 
show the explosion risks have been assessed. Once the 
necessary safety measures have been implemented and 
documented, the EPD is tested through a checklist, 
followed by further calibration if necessary 


--- Halaman 10 ---
What Is An Explosion Protection Document (EPD)
The EPD must include the following: 
⁄ The explosion risks have been determined and assessed.
⁄ Technical and organizational measures put in place to reduce the risk of explosions, and the measures used to 
lessen the effects of an explosion.
⁄ The operation of early warning devices.
⁄ Classified places (according to Schedule 1) where explosions may occur called hazardous zones and detail 
marking of areas as well as location.
⁄ Training and supervision procedures for anyone working in places where an explosive or flammable atmosphere 
may occur.
⁄ Operation of permits to work, maintenance, operational procedures and coordination between employers.
⁄ The properties of the substance that present an explosion hazard.
⁄ Means of escape in the event of an explosion or fire.
⁄ The selection and usage of proper equipment for use in hazardous zones (including certification and calibration 
documents); equipment is CE marked and in compliance with the ATEX product regulations.
⁄ How often the EPD is reviewed, renewed and the plans to renew in the future.

⁄An important section of the EPD document is the definition of 
Hazardous Area Zones and the requirements for equipment 
installed within these zones. 
⁄A hazardous zone is a location in which an explosive atmosphere 
consisting of combustible dusts, vapors or gases occurs during 
normal operation
⁄Hazardous zone classification will identify and document where 
these areas exist and will define them based on the likelihood and 
duration of an explosion atmosphere
⁄They are classified as follows: zone 0, 1 and 2 for an explosive gas 
and 20, 21 and 22 for dusts, with 0 and 20 indicating the highest 
hazard.

Frictional Heat and Hot-Work
⁄Frictional heat can be generated by material processing (sanding, routing, 
etc.) and also within equipment (bearings, conveyor belts, bucket 
elevators, and screw conveyors).
⁄Hot-Work (welding, brazing, etc.) describes maintenance activities that 
generate heat.
⁄Hot-Work permits are required for hot work in hazardous locations.

Static Electricity
⁄There are three main types of static electricity that can ignite dust clouds:
§Spark Discharges
§Bulking Brush (Cone) Discharges
§Propagating  Brush Discharges
⁄Spark discharges occur between two electrically conductive objects on a different 
electrical potential.  
⁄Electric charge builds on an object, and when the charged object touches an uncharged, 
conductive object, the electric charge bridges between the two objects. 
⁄A static shock when you touch a metal object is an example of a spark discharge.

Consider Consequences of Events
Once you have identified where fires and/or explosions can occur, you need to 
develop “What-If” scenarios.
Points to consider:
⁄ What are the safe operating parameters for the equipment?
⁄ What engineering controls (safeguards) are in place to mitigate the hazard, and 
what additional controls could be added?
⁄ What types of equipment and occupancies are near the location of the hazard?
⁄ What pieces of equipment are connected via ducts or conveyors to units where 
an explosion hazard exist?
⁄ What is the potential chain of events that could occur?
