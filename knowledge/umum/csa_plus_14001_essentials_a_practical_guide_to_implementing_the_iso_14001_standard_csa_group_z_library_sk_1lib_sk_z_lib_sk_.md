IntroductionBackground
In 
1996, the word “environment” had gained recognition through negative incidents such as the deadly 
Bhopal disaster, the Exxon Valdez oil spill, and the toxic waste dump in Love Canal. Environmental laws 
had been in place for decades in western nations, but business leaders were only beginning to 
understand that environmental impacts could cost their organizations dearly in terms of money (from 
fines and remediation) and reputation. Before 1996, the wait for environmental approvals could delay 
projects and was regarded as a substantial cost of doing business. Consequently, ISO 14001, launched in 
1996, soon became a valuable tool, but one that only really flourished in Europe and Asia (though it 
gained some traction in the automotive and forest products sectors in North America). ISO 14001 
enabled businesses to manage their environmental impacts effectively and more efficiently, while 
leveraging their certification as a competitive differentiator for their brand and reputation. Today, there 
are over 300 000 active certificates, and nearly 1000 have already met the requirements the 2016 
edition.
Today, zero waste is within the grasp of governments and industry, though 20 years ago the idea of 
recycling belonged only to dreamers. Going “beyond compliance” and being “green” were themes that 
leading management consultants were peddling with only limited success to the multinational 
companies, such as those in the chemical industry, who had a lot at stake. Their voluntary industry-wide 
responsible care initiative (designed to improve the environment, health and safety, and emergency 
response and communications) quite possibly saved the industry in the west from the “not in my 
backyard” (NIMBY) syndrome. Leaders understood that the accidents of one “bad apple” could tarnish 
the reputation of all. Most businesses now have a designated environmental or sustainability 
representative. Some of these representatives sit at the highest levels of their organizations. The 
realization that the environment and society must be considered when economic development is 
planned has become widespread.
This new edition of the ISO 14001 implementation handbook can help organizations move toward 
sustainability using the “Plan-Do-Check-Act” approach that has proven successful for decades in many 
sectors.How 
to use this handbookThis 
implementation handbook is intended to be used alongside CAN/CSA-ISO 14001, which is available 
through the CSA Group website http://shop.csa.ca/en/canada/page/home.
This handbook recognizes that many organizations will be managing their environmental aspects either 
through an existing EMS or just as a requirement of doing business. Consequently, each chapter begins 
with a handy list to remind you of what your organization might already have in place to help you get 
started in developing or enhancing your EMS.

How do organizations approach environmental management?
In general, many organizations only do what is required to comply with the law. They take advantage of 
what economists term “externalities” to profit. The cost of pollution is born by others – often the  
public – in what is known as the “tragedy of the commons” (when shared resources such as our water 
bodies and atmosphere become polluted). Society as a whole, rather than the organizations who 
generated the pollution, pays the price through poorer health, higher healthcare costs, a degraded 
environment, loss of species and biodiversity, and other effects. Such indifference is at odds with the 
“polluter pays principle”, which resonates with most people.
Environmental laws at the federal and provincial levels were developed to drive improved 
environmental management. Most of the existing environmental regulations in the US were first 
developed in the 1970s and marked a change in how organizations were required to behave. Canada 
lagged a little behind the US and took a somewhat different approach than the American command- 
and-control style, environmental-media-focused regulations. What is important, however, is that 
organizations in Canada were also forced to manage their environmental aspects. Government 
inspection still ensures organizations are complying.
For many organizations, regulatory compliance is their main driver. These organizations see pollution 
control or prevention as a cost. However, since the 1990s, organizations have increasingly been 
concerned about their liability. Events such as Bhopal and Exxon Valdez led organizations to focus on 
risk management. Some considered building a competitive advantage by introducing “beyond 
compliance” initiatives. Could having a green and sustainable image possibly generate value? These and 
other considerations may have provided the impetus to develop ISO 14001:1996 (which closely follows 
the UK standard BS 7750 first published in 1992).
ISO 14001 was launched in 1996 and was well received in Europe (possibly because of the 
Environmental Management and Audit Scheme [EMAS]) and then in Asia. Motivated primarily by the 
need to gain or hold market access, large consumer product companies encouraged their supply chain 
manufacturers to adopt ISO 14001. The US and Canada were slower to take the initiative, asking instead 
“Do we have to do it?” In Canada, the forestry sector, followed by the automotive sector, and then the 
chemical sector have become more robust supporters of ISO 14001, and the support continues to grow. 
This is primarily a result of demands by customers — certainly a key interested party! In 2004,  
ISO 14001 was revised slightly. In 2016, the quality and environmental management systems standard  
(ISO 9001) underwent major revisions and there will soon be a standard for safety management 
systems — ISO 45001. One reason for the revisions was to make the integration of these management 
systems more seamless, but there were many other improvements as well.

blishing an EMS is not a solo endeavour but a team effort. You will need leadership and

Over 300 000 organizations have been certified to ISO 14001 in the 20 years since its launch. More are

As with any kind of organizational change, successful outcomes can best be supported by strong 
organizational champions and sponsors. A champion is someone who is respected by both workers and 
management, has a passion for the cause, and is willing to be the face of the system. A sponsor is 
someone in top management who will continue to advocate for the allocation of time and resources to 
support the system. A sponsor has the authority and power to make decisions at the highest levels. In 
some organizations, the champion and sponsor may be the same person. In addition to identifying 
champions and sponsors, you may want to identify people who have experience implementing  
change — especially if those people have had experience implementing other standards.
Building the business case
• Take a baseline of the year prior to implementation and gather the costs of the following: 
– waste disposal
– hazardous waste management
– wastewater disposal (and treatment, if applicable)
– air emissions fees
– water
– electricity
– fuels
• Compile the costs savings potential for 10 to 15% reduction of the above. Use these annual savings 
to present the benefits of implementing an EMS

Understanding the organization and its context
When introducing any new program, an organization first needs to know where it is before proceeding 
to where it wants to go. What will make your environmental management system appropriate to your 
needs, effective, and efficient? Some factors you can control to an extent, such as your location, the 
markets you serve, your products and/or services, and your vendors and suppliers. Factors outside your 
control may include customers, investors, insurers, regulators, and third parties affected by the 
organization. Regulatory requirements and other industry standards, codes of practice, and the like can 
influence the environmental management system. And there are other factors as well.

Has your organization ever experienced these risks or come close to experiencing them? That negative

How can you manage these potential risks in such a way as to turn them into sustainable opportunities? 
We suggest there is value in a thorough and repeated evaluation of the organization in accordance with 
Clause 4.1. See discussion of Clause 6 for more insights.
Understanding the needs and expectations of interested parties
Fortunately, most organizations will already be managing their environmental aspects — either through 
CAN/CSA-ISO 14001 or their own in-house means — so Clause 4.1 might be satisfied by the systematic 
tabulation described in Clause 4.2 of the external and internal issues that are relevant to its purpose 
and that affect its ability to achieve the intended outcomes of its environmental management system. 
The tabulation should include opportunities, not just risks, threats, and requirements. The aim is to end 
up knowing clearly what CAN/CSA-ISO 14001 calls “compliance obligations” — mandatory and voluntary 
requirements that your organization is committed to meet and that could provide strategic advantages 
such as a sustainable or green image, buy-in from external supporters/partners and interested parties, 
and market differentiation.
Determining the scope of the environmental management system
The boundaries of the full life cycle of an organization’s activities, products, and services can be very 
far-reaching (consider the location of users of gasoline, natural gas, fertilizers, coolants, and inert gas, 
just to start) and the system should manage, and be applicable to, all associated environmental aspects. 
Clause 4.3 provides a list of considerations that should be taken into account when establishing the 
scope of the EMS. The reader is encouraged to approach this exercise with a broad view that includes 
strategic advantages and not focus on the minimum an organization can “get away with”. Since the 
scope will be made available to interested parties, it should be specific and avoid misleading language 
that might lead to unrealistic expectations.
Environmental management system
Environmental management systems are intended to enhance the organization’s environmental and 
hence overall performance, and the EMS likely will do so if the knowledge gained in following  
Clauses 4.1 and 4.2 is applied and if CAN/CSA-ISO 14001 is viewed as intended and not just as a set of 
minimum requirements. When first establishing an environmental management system, an organization 
could begin where there is obvious benefit (for example, by focusing on immediate cost savings or 
regulatory compliance related to its significant environmental aspects). As the environmental 
management system takes shape, procedures, programs, and technologies can be put in place to 
further improve environmental performance. As the environmental management system matures, 
environmental considerations can be integrated into all business decisions.Leadership

(R) is often represented as the product of likelihood (L) and severity (S) (i.e., R = L × S). In some 
cases, the effects of exposure are added, often in biological contexts. There is a wide variety of ways to 
subdivide likelihood and severity, resulting in highly elaborate risk matrices. If your organization already 
has a risk-determining matrix, it is probably best to apply that tool since it is already accepted and 
understood.
Since the purpose of a risk matrix is to determine significance and its priority, a simple matrix will be 
easiest and will likely suffice. Likelihood could be “rare”, “sometimes”, or “common” (1, 2, 3), and 
severity could be “acceptable”, “undesirable”, or “unacceptable” (1, 2, 3). Risk ranking would then be 
either 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, or 9. Clearly rankings of 9 and 6 will pose the most significant risk. Possibly any 
unacceptable occurrence might be deemed significant. The choice is up to the organization.
It usually pays off to add the human perspective and filter the results according to how reasonable they 
are rather than blindly accepting the math.

Do 
all significant environmental aspects require controls?
An organization might look at the risks it faces with and without controls. Some risks are simply too 
great to leave to chance. For example, the management of cooling water at a nuclear reactor will need 
to be carefully controlled with a host of backup measures just in case. At the other extreme, sending 
waste to a landfill or wastewater to a wastewater treatment plant in the vicinity might seem adequate 
or normal business practice. Simple monitoring and confirmation that acceptance criteria have been 
met might suffice. Preventing the waste streams, however, might provide a business opportunity. So 
taking action (to control) has a cost but also a potential benefit. Taking no action might not have a cost, 
but it will also not have a benefit.
What important considerations come into play when planning actions?
In the real world, not all significant environmental aspects can be eliminated or even reduced to a low 
risk. Organizations will need to take into consideration
• technological options
• financial implications
• operational factors
• business requirements
How to manage planning action?
If the advice to use a spreadsheet to record environmental aspects is heeded, then it is a simple matter 
to add a few columns to the sheet where management action can be planned and recorded. 
Management actions will include monitoring, communication, training, procedures, investment in 
technology, outsourcing, or any combination of these. Each significant aspect should have an associated 
management tool. Many organizations will extend this process to the environmental aspects that have 
not been deemed significant as well, recognizing that most of these will require low levels of control or 
will be controlled as a consequence of the establishment of procedures aimed at managing significant 
environmental aspects.
Once an organization understands its environmental aspects and its compliance obligations, especially 
its significant environmental aspects and related obligations, the process of managing these within the 
environmental management system can be planned. It should be emphasized that there are not only 
risks but opportunities, and a smart, well-designed environmental management system will take 
advantage of these opportunities. Opportunities arise from the growing insistence among interested 
parties for corporate sustainability. There are also the benefits reaped by organizations when they 
mitigate climate change by generating their own renewable energy. Minimizing impacts through climate 
change adaptation could mean the difference between continuing in business and failure.Environmental 
objectivesTo 
improve environmental performance, an organization will establish some environmental objectives. 
What part of the organization should do this? Any relevant level or relevant function. Often a function 
that generates waste will have the most opportunities, but it is also useful to consider upstream 
objectives related to
• preventing waste generation
• conserving resources and energy

Environmental objectivesThe 
organization shall establish environmental objectives at relevant functions and levels, taking into 
account the organization’s significant environmental aspects and associated compliance obligations, 
and considering its risks and opportunities.
The environmental objectives shall be: 
a) consistent with the environmental policy;
b) measurable (if practicable);
c) monitored;
d) communicated;
e) updated as appropriate.
The organization shall maintain documented information on the environmental objectives.6.2.2 
Planning actions to achieve environmental objectivesWhen 
planning how to achieve its environmental objectives, the organization shall determine: 
a) what will be done;
b) what resources will be required;
c) who will be responsible;
d) when it will be completed;
e) how the results will be evaluated, including indicators for monitoring progress toward 
achievement of its measurable environmental objectives (see 9.1.1).
The organization shall consider how actions to achieve its environmental objectives can be integrated 
into the organization’s business processes.
Objectives can be near-term (when it is clear what is needed) or long-term (involving some R&D): a 
combination of these works best. Many functions might share the same objective, such as waste 
minimization. Delegating to lower levels will harness greater power within the organization.
Of course, the information related to objectives and everything associated with them needs to be 
documented, and these documents must be maintained.Planning 
to achieve environmental objectivesTo 
achieve their objectives, organizations need to determine the “Who? What? Where? When? and 
How?” of plans to achieve the objectives. To avoid reinventing the wheel and proving ineffective, 
organizations need to consider how to integrate the plans into broader business processes. One clear 
example is that every company does a reasonable job of tracking financial flows and totals. There is no 
need to reproduce this function. Similarly, often production areas have bulletin boards showing 
productivity and other metrics/indicators. These are great tools to borrow in order to raise awareness, 
communicate, enlist ideas for continual improvement, measure and report on performance, and so on.
