COURSE INTRODUCTION  
 
THE COMPEX COMPETENCY SCHEME 
To work safely in place where there is a possibility, however small, that an explosive atmosphere may be present, 
demands that appropriate work methods and equipment are used. These requirements can be met by compliance 
with the standards and legislation which have been developed specifically for this purpose.  
 
In the UK, the Electricity at Work Regulations (1989) imposed requirements to employ only competent persons to 
undertake electrical installation and maintenance work. Other countries have enacted similar legislation. 
Companies in the petrochemical industry have normally provided in-house training for employees who were 
expected to work in potentially explosive atmospheres. Some contractors and companies send their personnel to 
receive this essential training from an approved training centre. 
 
In 1990, The Engineering Equipment and Materials Users ’Association (EEMUA) prepared guidelines and together 
with JTL, the training company set up by the Electrical Contractors Association, training schedules were developed 
and the criteria laid down with which training and assessment centres would need to comply, forming the basis of 
the EEMUA CompEx scheme. JTLimited, as the CompEx Certification Body, is accredited to IEC / ISO 17024 and 
those who successfully complete the training and pass the written and practical assessment  are awarded a 
CompEx “Certificate of Core Competence”. 
 
CompEx (Competency in Explosive atmospheres) is an accredited international scheme that validates core 
competency for safe working in potentially hazardous, flammable or explosive atmospheres. CompEx de termines 
Core Competency by checking both the underpinning knowledge by examination and practical skills and 
ability by assessment. 
 
The main object of CompEx training and competency validation is to enhance the knowledge and practical ability of 
operatives who have already gained a basic knowledge of electrical/mechanical installation, through working within 
the contracting and installation sector of the electrical industry and to certify their competence. Experience 
gained in maritime activities or service overseas may also give a candidate the necessary prior knowledge.

1.2 Gases and Vapors 
 
1.2.1 Explosive Atmospheres 
The gases with which the course is more concerned are those which are flammable when mixed with air. Due to 
the presence of oxygen in air, the mixture burns fiercely if ignited. The resulting hot burning gas rapidly expands, 
developing into an explosion. 
Significant amount of research and development has been undertaken in order to gain vital information relative to  
combinations of gas and air, such as might be present throughout manufacturing and process industries in order to 
minimize the risk of an unwanted gas ignition. 
This course covers explosives atmospheres where the fuel is gas or vapour.  
The International Standards for Explosive atmospheres is IEC 60079. 
 
 
 
 
1.2.2 IGNITION REQUIREMENTS  
 
THE FIRE TRIANGLE 
The fire triangle is used as a method to assist in the better understanding of the conditions required to create an 
ignition or explosion. The three sides of the triangle represent the three conditions which are necessary.  
1. Fuel:   This can be in the form of a gas, vapour, mist or dust 
2. Oxygen:   Approximately 21% by volume in normal air. 
3. Source of Ignition:    This can be an arc, spark, naked flame or hot surfaces

Combustion will take place if all three elements are present, the gas/air mixture is within certain limits, and the

 Removal of one or more elements by isolation or separation of the source of ignition from the gas / air 
mixture. 
 Allow the three elements to co -exist and ensure that the energy of the sou rce of ignition is maintained 
below specific values. 
 Allow an explosion to take place inside and contain it within a robust enclosure.

1.2.3 CHARACTERISTICS OF GASES AND VAPOURS 
IEC 60079-20:1996 Electrical apparatus for explosive gas atmospheres –Part 20: gives data for flammable gases 
and vapours, relating to the use of electrical apparatus. 
Some of the characteristics of gases we will consider follows;  
Flammable (explosive) limits, ignition temperature, ignition energy (ignition current), density, flashpoint.  
Extracts from IEC 60079-20-1 are attached herein. 
 
FLAMMABLE (EXPLOSIVE) LIMITS 
Mixture of a flammable gas within certain percentages with air (21% oxygen) burn if ignited. If there is too much or 
too little flammable material the mixture does not ignite.  
Combustion will only occur if the concentration of the flammable mixture of fuel (gas or vapour) and air is within 
certain limits. These limits are the ‘lower explosive limit’  (LEL), and the ‘upper explosive limit’ (UEL) , and 
between these limits is the flammable range.  
Definition: 
 L.E.L. the concentration of flammable gas, vapour or mist in air, below which an  explosive gas atmosphere will 
not be formed.  
When the percentage of gas by volume is below this limit, the mixture is too weak to burn, i.e. insufficient fuel 
and/or too much air. 
 U.E.L. the concentration of flammable gas, vapour or mist in air, above which an explosive gas atmosphere will 
not be formed. 
When the percentage of gas by volume is above this limit, the mixture is too rich to burn, i.e. insufficient air and/or 
too much fuel. 
 
 
Different gases or vapours have different flammable limits and the greater the differe nce between the LEL and 
the UEL (the flammable range), the more dangerous the gas.  
Operational safety is generally achieved by operation well below the LEL.

The ignition temperature of a material is the minimum temperature , under prescribed conditions, at which the 
material can ignite and sustain combustion, when mixed with air at normal pressure, without the ignition initiated 
by any spark or flame. 
Ignition temperature is ‘the minimum temperature at which a flammable material will spontaneously ignite’.  There 
is sufficient thermal energy to cause ignition.

1.3 GAS GROUP AND GAS SUBDIVISIONS 
Gases can be grouped into Group I and Group II.  
Gas Group I refer to gases encountered in the underground mining industries represented by methane 
(firedamp). 
Gas Group II applies to gases encountered in the surface industries (non-mining industries), and are divided 
based on the ignition risk properties of the atmosphere. 
Classification is based on experimental methods. From the experimental data, Gas subdivision II which applies to 
gases encountered in the surface industries (non-mining industries), are divided based on the ignition risk 
properties of the atmosphere into IIA, IIB, and IIC. 
 
1.3.1 TEST METHODS: DETERMINATION OF GAS SUBDIVISION (GAS GROUP) 
Gases may be categorised into subdivisions by one (or both) of two test methods: 
1. Subdivisions based on Minimum Ignition Current (MIC). MIC is of greatest value in determining standards 
for intrinsically safe apparatus. 
2. Subdivisions based on Maximum Experimental Safe Gap (MESG). MESG is based on work undertaken to 
develop flameproof equipment. 
 
The first, relevant to Intrinsically Safe apparatus, involved determining the minimum ignition energy (MIE) which 
would ignite the most explosive concentration of the gas in air.  
An inductive spark discharge apparatus is specified i n the standards, and this is used to measure the Minimum 
Igniting Current (MIC) ratio, which is the ratio of the gas MIC to that of laboratory methane.  
 
Equipment Group IIA is required for MIC > 80% (85 mA) = 68 mA 
Equipment Group IIB is required for 45% ≤ MIC ≤ 80%     = (38.3 to 68 mA) 
Equipment Group IIC is required for MIC < 45%  (<38.2 mA) 
The other method determines the ‘maximum experimental safe gap’ (MESG) using an 8 flameproof litre sphere 
situated inside a gas-tight enclosure.

1.6 AREA CLASSIFICATION - Gases 
Area Classification is concerned with the classification of areas into zones, where there may be a risk of ignition 
and possibly explosion due to the presence of flammable gas or vapour, mixed with ai r under normal atmospheric 
conditions. The applicable international standard is IEC 60079-10. 
The Area Classification normally is carried out by those who understand the relevance and significance of 
properties of flammable materials and those who are fami liar with the process and the equipment along with safety, 
electrical, mechanical and other qualified engineering personnel. 
A hazardous area is defined as: An area in which an explosive gas atmosphere is present , or may be expected 
to be present, in quant ities such as to require special precautions for the construction, installation and use of 
equipment. 
A non-hazardous area is defined as: 
‘An area in which an explosive gas atmosphere is not expected to be present  in quantities such as to require 
special p recautions for the construction, installation and use of equipment.’ Some equipment rooms situated in 
zoned area are made safe by introducing air under slight pressure drawn from a non -hazardous area. Entry is 
made via an airlock.

1.6.1 ZONES  
Area classification, in respect of gases, is concerned with the classification of area s into zones. Zoning is a means 
of representing the probability, frequency and duration of an explosive gas atmosphere, based on the 
identification and consideration of each and every source of release in the given areas of an installation, including 
the: 
• Grade of release (continuous, primary or secondary)  
• Presence of artificial ventilation 
• Extent and volume of release  
 
IEC and ATEX Workplace Directives use the zone concept to define hazardous areas. The three Zones represent 
the risk in terms of the prob ability, frequency and duration of a release.  Zoning will have a bearing on the 
selection of the type of explosion protected equipment which may be used.  
The three Zones, as defined in IEC60079 -10-1 (Classification of hazardous areas - Explosive gas atmos pheres), 
are as follows: 
 
ZONE 0  
Is a place in which, an explosive gas atmosphere consisting of a mixture with air of flammable substances in the 
form of gas, vapour or mist’s present  continuously or for long periods or frequently. The area is outlined a nd infilled 
by circles.  
Example: Zone 0 is found typically in the space above the liquid in a mixing vat or storage tank.

1.7 EQUIPMENT SELECTION  
1.7.1 BY EQUIPMENT GROUP 
Equipment for use in potentially explosive atmospheres is divided into Groups and Categorie s. The group 
allocation for mining and surface industries are separate.  
Group I equipment is a designation given to equipment designed and tested for use underground in the mining 
industry (hazards are methane (‘firedamp’) & coal dust)   
Group II equipment is a designation given to equipment designed and tested for use above ground for surface 
industries where risks from ignition of a wide range of flammable gases may be present. Group II is subdivided 
into IIA, IIB and IIC Typical gases: propane, ethylene, and hydrogen 

  
Group III is for surface industries with dust hazards and is subdivided into IIIA, IIIB and IIIC Flyings, non-
conductive dusts, and conductive dusts 
This course deals with Group II 
The equipment group or sub-group will be marked on the equipment nameplate:  
Equipment marked just II may be used in all hazards (IIA, IIB, IIC). 
Equipment marked IIA can only be used in IIA hazards. 
Equipment marked IIB can be used in IIB and IIA hazards. 
Equipment marked IIC can be used in all hazards (IIA.IIB, IIC).

1.7.2  BY TEMPERATURE CLASSIFICATION: ‘T’ CLASS 
Temperature classification is a system of classification of equipment into one of six (6) temperatures ranging from 
850C up to 4500C. 
Temperature classification (‘T Rating”) is based on the maximum temperature which any relevant part of the 
equipment which may be in contact with an explosive gas can reach, when operated within its maximum 
designed rating and ambient temperature normally not exceeding 40oC.  
Electrical equipment must be selected to ensure that the surface temperature produced by the equipment 
(indicated by the ‘T-rating’) will not exceed the ignition temperature of the flammable atmosphere which may be 
present around the equipment.  
Equipment temperature class marking (‘T-rating’) and its equivalent 0C 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
In the UK, ambient temperature range for Ex equipment is from -20°C to + 40°C, unless otherwise marked on the 
equipment The equipment ambient rating must be compatible with the actual environment ambient temperature.  
T-rating temperatures are based on the maximum ambient for the equipment, and this is the temperature tha t must 
be used in any temperature rise calculations.  
Equipment for use in hot climates, such as the Middle Eastern countries, will usually require ambient ratings 
greater than 40°C.  Equipment for use in colder (arctic) climates will require a much lower limit to the ambient 
temperature range, which may be as low as -50°C. 
 
Such equipment would need special materials and expensive testing for certification.

1.7.3  BY IP RATING (INGRESS PROTECTION CODE, IP__) 
Enclosures of electrical equipment are classified according to their ability to resist the ingress of solid 
objects and water by means of a system of numbers known as the ‘Ingress Protection’ Code. This 
consists of the letters IP + two numbers, e.g. IP56. 
The first number, in the range 0-6, indicates the degree of protection against solid objects, and the 
higher the number the smaller the solid object that is prevented from entering the enclosure. Zero (0) 
indicates no protection, and 6 indicates that the equipment is dust-tight. 
The second number, ranging from 0-8, identifies the level of protection against water entering the 
enclosure, from 0 – ‘no protection’ to 8 – ‘withstands indefinite immersion a specified at depth’.
