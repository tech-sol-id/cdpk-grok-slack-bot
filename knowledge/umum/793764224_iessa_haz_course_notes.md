Misunderstanding the environment: 3
Misunderstanding the environment: 
• Sam is tasked with replacing a piece of equipment that has 
been malfunctioning. The equipment is rated as “Ex” or 
“Explosion Proof”, meaning it’s designed to prevent any sparks 
or heat that could ignite the flammable gases present in the 
environment.
• However, Sam misunderstands the “Ex” rating. He assumes it 
means the equipment is resistant to explosions, not that it’s 
designed to prevent them. Based on this misunderstanding, he 
decides to replace the malfunctioning equipment with a non-Ex 
rated equipment that he believes is more robust and explosion-
resistant.
• The non-Ex rated equipment that Sam installs is not designed 
to prevent sparks or excessive heat. This increases the risk of a 
potential ignition of the flammable gases, leading to a possible 
explosion. This could endanger the safety of the entire oil rig 
and its personnel.

Ignorance and lack of training or adhering to safety 
standards: 4
Ignorance and lack of training or 
adhering to safety standards: 
• A chemical plant where two workers, Alex and Bob, are 
responsible for monitoring and maintaining the safety of the 
plant.
• One day, Alex sees a warning light on one of the control panels. 
He remembers that during his training, a similar light indicated 
a minor issue with one of the non-critical systems. So, he 
decides not to report it immediately and plans to fix it during 
his next shift.
• However, the light actually indicates a significant leak in one of 
the chemical tanks. Bob, who was trained more recently, knows 
this. But since Alex didn’t mention it, Bob assumes that Alex has 
already initiated the necessary safety protocols.
• The misunderstanding between Alex and Bob leads to a delay 
in addressing the chemical leak. This could potentially escalate 
into a major incident, endangering the safety of the entire plant 
and its workers.

Knowing better than experts: 5
Knowing better than experts: 
• The Dunning-Kruger effect is like when an engineer or 
accountant first starts out in their field. They might feel overly 
confident about their skills because they aren’t fully aware of 
the depth and complexity of their profession yet. This 
overconfidence can lead to mistakes or oversights.
• On the other hand, a highly experienced engineer or 
accountant might underestimate their skills. They understand 
the vastness of their field and know there’s always more to 
learn. This could lead them to undervalue their expertise.
• In both cases, the key is continuous learning and self-
awareness. It’s important to accurately assess one’s skills and 
knowledge, and to always be open to growth and improvement. 
This can lead to better decision-making and performance in 
their respective fields. Remember, it’s better to ask a question 
than to assume and risk a potential misunderstanding.

Introduction to Explosion Prevention
• Fire is a rapid oxidation of material releasing heat, light and various chemical products. 
• The fire triangle/tetrahedron describes the conditions that have to be met in order a fire can start: 
(1) flammable material (Fuel), (2) oxygen, (3) energy to ignite the fire (Heat).
• All material capable of an exothermic oxidation reaction (Chemical release that involves the 
release of energy in the form of heal or light) has to be considered as flammable. 
This can be:
• gases such as butane, propane, methane, carbon monoxide, hydrogen,
• liquids such as fuels, solvents, oils, greases, paints and thinners,
• solids such as wood, coal, plastics, metals, food.
• Oxygen is usually available in sufficient quantities in our air to get a fire started and to sustain it. 
Fires may however start much easier and may be more powerful in terms of flame volume and 
released energy, if the oxygen content of the surrounding atmosphere is increased, e.g. when an 
oxygen cylinder leaks or bursts or when oxygen releasing substances (e.g. peroxides) are present.
• The needed ignition energy can be very low (usually with gases) and can be quite high, which is 
usually the case with solids. Liquids are often somewhere in between. However the ignition of 
solids or aerosols depends also on the particle size: fine dusts of e.g. aluminium or flour mixed 
with air can explode easily.
• An explosion is a rapid increase in volume and release of energy in an extreme manner, usually 
with the generation of high temperatures and the release of gases. An explosion creates a shock 
wave. Does the created shock wave exceed the velocity of sound we talk about a detonation; is 
the velocity lower the term deflagration is used.

IP Rating
• IP rating stands for Ingress Protection 
rating, and it is a standard used to 
indicate how well an electronic device or 
enclosure is protected against intrusion 
from solid objects like dust and liquid.
• The IP rating is composed of two 
numbers. The first number indicates the 
level of protection against solid objects, 
while the second number indicates the 
level of protection against liquids.
• For example, an IP68 rating means that 
the device or enclosure is completely 
dust-tight and can withstand immersion in 
water for extended periods, while an IP44 
rating means that the device or enclosure 
is protected against solid objects larger 
than 1mm in size and against splashing 
water.

Flameproof (Ex d): This technique involves enclosing electrical 
equipment in a housing that is designed to contain and cool 
any explosions that may occur inside the enclosure. The 
enclosure is made of strong and durable materials that can 
withstand the pressure generated by the explosion.
Increased safety (Ex e): This technique involves using 
equipment that is designed to prevent sparks or high 
temperatures from occurring, by incorporating additional 
measures such as increased insulation and reduced voltage.
Intrinsic safety (Ex i): This technique involves limiting the 
amount of electrical energy that is available in the circuit to 
a level that is too low to ignite the surrounding atmosphere. 
This is achieved through the use of intrinsically safe barriers 
or isolators.
Dust ignition-proof (Ex tD): This technique is specifically 
designed for use in environments where combustible dust is 
present. Equipment using this technique is designed to 
prevent the buildup of static electricity, which could cause 
dust ignition.
Encapsulation (Ex m): This technique involves coating the 
electrical components of the equipment in a protective 
compound to prevent any ignition sources from escaping. 
This technique is commonly used in environments where 
flammable gases or liquids are present.

Zone Classification & 
Applications
The relevant national legislation on hazardous locations requires the employer to classify 
potentially hazardous locations.
In the relevant national legislation, certain hazardous locations are specified for all underground 
coal mines, but other locations and hazardous areas in other mines shall be determined by the 
classification process.
The area classification should be carried out when the initial process, instrumentation line diagrams 
and initial layout plans are available and confirmed before plant start-up. Furthermore, it is the 
employer’s responsibility to ensure that the area classification remains up-to-date; either by reviews 
at regular intervals or as part of change management, to ensure that all modifications to the plant 
or mine that could affect the area classification are taken into account.
Classification of hazardous areas or locations shall be carried out by either a qualified engineer, or 
a competent person who is familiar with the process and equipment, as well as with the safety, 
electrical, and mechanical processes.

Zone Classification & 
Applications
• Area classification is a method of analysing and classifying the environment 
where explosive gas atmospheres may occur so as to facilitate the proper 
selection and installation of equipment to be used safely in that 
environment. The classification also takes into account the ignition 
characteristics of the gas or vapour such as ignition energy (gas group) and 
ignition temperature (temperature class).
• It is rarely possible by a simple examination of a plant or plant design to 
decide which parts of the plant can be equated to the six zonal definitions 
(zones 0, 1, 2, 20, 21 and 22). A more detailed approach is therefore 
necessary and this involves the analysis of the basic possibility of an 
explosive gas atmosphere occurring.

Zone Classification & 
Applications
1. Identify the source of release
2. Identify the Type of Zone
3. Extent of Zone
Since an explosive gas atmosphere can exist only if a 
flammable gas or vapour is present with air, it is necessary 
to decide if any of these flammable materials can exist in 
the area concerned. Each item of process equipment (for 
example, tank, pump, pipeline, vessel, etc.) should be 
considered as a potential source of release of flammable 
material.
The likelihood of the presence of an explosive gas 
atmosphere depends mainly on the grade of release and 
the ventilation. This is identified as a zone. Zones are 
recognized as: zone 0, zone 1, zone 2 and the non-
hazardous area.
NOTE 1 
A continuous grade of release normally leads to a zone 0, 
a primary grade to zone 1 and a secondary grade to zone 
2 (see Annex B).
NOTE 2 
Where zones created by adjacent sources of release 
overlap and are of different zonal classification, the higher 
classification criteria will apply in the area of overlap. 
Where overlapping zones are of the same classification, 
this common classification will normally apply.
The extent of the zone depends on the estimated or calculated distance over 
which an explosive atmosphere exists before it disperses to a concentration in 
air below its lower explosive limit with an appropriate safety factor. Expert 
advice should be sought when assessing the area of spread of gas or vapour
before dilution to below its lower explosive limit.
